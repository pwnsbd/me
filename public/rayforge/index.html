RayForge

Pawan Subedi · started 12 September 2026 · still in progress

A VR optics bench where light obeys real physics. Pick up a lens, shine a torch through it, and look through the glass with your own eyes.

Built in Unreal Engine 5.8 as a C++ plugin with Python scene tooling, running on Meta Quest 3 through OpenXR. I build it on my own.

Why I'm building it

Optics is taught with diagrams on a flat page, but lenses are spatial. You understand a magnifier by moving it, or a focal point by watching a beam pinch together on a rail. RayForge puts a real optical bench in VR: rails, holders, lenses cut from catalogue glass, lasers and torches. Every beam and every view through the glass comes from a tested optics engine, not from art tricks.

The long-term goal is a lab where any lens can be described as data (radii, thickness, glass) and is correct in the maths, in the beam and in what your eye sees, without writing new code.

Three rules hold the project together:

  1. Each optical law is written once. Shaders, visuals and Python read results from the core. They never redo the physics.
  2. A lens is data. A prescription of faces, gaps and catalogue glass. The mesh, the beam and the view are all derived from it.
  3. Nothing ships untested. Every new surface or lens shape is checked against an analytic or catalogue value before it appears in a scene.

What works today

Everything below runs in the headset now. The names in grey are the C++ classes.

  • Lens as data. Ordered faces with signed radii, gaps and an aperture, validated before use. Presets are real N-BK7 singlets. A glass catalogue gives refractive index from Sellmeier coefficients at the d-line (587.56 nm). FForgedLensPrescription
  • One element tracer. Traces a ray through any stack of faces: singlets, doublets, an achromat. It handles plane, spherical and conic surfaces, Fresnel reflection and transmission splits, total internal reflection, and absorption at the lens rim. FForgedOpticalElement
  • Scene tracer. A registry of every optic in the level does broadphase culling, then an analytic narrowphase finds the exact hit. Lenses, mirrors and receivers all answer through two small interfaces. UForgedOpticalRegistrySubsystem
  • Seeing through glass. For each eye, rays from the headset are traced backwards through the real lens solver, so the world appears magnified, flipped or shrunk exactly as the optics say. A sparse grid with analytic ray differentials keeps it within the Quest 3 frame budget. AForgedWorldOpticalView
  • Lights and torch. Lasers, point lights, a candle and a handheld torch. The torch traces its own beam volume through optics, and a rays view shows every traced ray. Rays that miss every optic turn grey. AForgedFlashlightActor
  • Bench and holders. A graduated rail with holders that seat lights and lenses on one optical axis. You grab at arm's length, slide along the rail, and use a hold gesture to detach. AForgedLensHolderActor
  • Meta hands in VR. A VR pawn whose hands are driven by the Meta controllers, with pointer-click UI for the stations. Interaction SDK v207
  • Two-station lab. A teaching room built entirely by Python scripts: a lens introduction, bench assembly and an optical breadboard. Each station's see-through view switches on when you enter its zone. scripts/stations/

How the code is laid out

The core module depends only on Unreal's Core and CoreUObject, so all the optics maths runs headless in tests without a world or a GPU. The actors module turns those results into things you can see and hold.

Things that broke, and how I fixed them

  1. Real see-through optics at headset frame rate

    Tracing every pixel through a thick lens, for two eyes, is far too slow on a mobile headset.

    Fix: trace a sparse grid of eye rays through the real solver and interpolate with Hermite patches using analytic ray differentials. A per-frame ray budget spreads the work across frames.

  2. The image sheared when you moved the lens

    Rays traced over several frames were all compared against one recorded lens pose.

    Fix: record the pose per slice of rays. The test now moves the lens between steps, so the bug can't come back quietly.

  3. Two answers to one geometry question

    Entry testing and the core trace chose different roots for grazing rays on concave faces, so they disagreed.

    Fix: both now call one shared helper. That became a project rule: when two code paths answer the same question, they share the code.

  4. A black background that survived two fixes

    Each fix passed its maths tests, but the headset still showed black, and nothing showed which render stage failed.

    Fix: rendering tasks now ship with a debug view switch (per pass, coverage, sky only), so a single look in VR finds the failing stage.

  5. Torch power and beam brightness were the same number

    Setting power through ray intensity would have made beams fade with distance.

    Fix: split the two. Intensity is a display weight only, and power in watts has its own path through the tracer.

What's next

  • Light that adds up: power in watts per ray, emitter profiles as data, and spectral light so glass splits colour (dispersion).
  • Lens Forger: forge and validate any lens in the headset from radii, thickness, glass and aperture.
  • One renderer path: retire the old shader tracers in favour of the core, with pixel-level validation of what the eye sees.
  • Quest budget and authoring: split the lens actor into components, lock the Quest 3 performance budget, and write a guide for adding new optics.
